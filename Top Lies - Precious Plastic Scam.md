# Precious Plastic: Twenty Claims Under Scrutiny

*A public record of disputed promises, technical shortcomings, and concerns raised by people who have built, repaired, supported, or attempted to operate Precious Plastic machinery.*

## Executive summary

Precious Plastic has built its public identity around an attractive proposition: open-source machines, accessible recycling, a thriving international community, and viable small businesses turning waste into products. The idea deserves scrutiny precisely because it has persuaded people to invest time, money, professional effort, and trust.

This article examines twenty recurring claims against the practical requirements of machinery design, manufacturing, occupational safety, commercial production, community governance, and transparent accounting. It incorporates the author's direct experience and reports from other practitioners. The central criticism is not that small-scale recycling is impossible, or that every participant acts in bad faith. It is that promotional statements have, in the author's assessment, repeatedly exceeded what the publicly available designs, records, and operating results can substantiate.

Several allegations below—including censorship, preferential marketplace treatment, misleading attribution, financial claims, and the treatment of contributors—are serious and disputed matters. They are presented as the author's account or as reported concerns, not as independently adjudicated findings. Figures cited from past posts are leads for verification, not audited statistics. Links and primary records should be evaluated alongside any response from the people and organizations named.

The burden of proof matters. If machines are advertised as safe, capable, economical, and suitable for a business, their providers should be able to publish complete designs, test results, conformity documentation, operating costs, and evidence of sustained commercial use. Branding, videos, member counts, and anecdotes are not substitutes.

## The project's public promise—and the gap under examination

Precious Plastic brought broad visibility to local recycling and helped people discover fabrication and plastics processing. Its early materials inspired workshops, experiments, and contributions from volunteers. Those achievements are real parts of the story.

So are the consequences when an educational prototype is presented as a commercially credible machine. Industrial recycling involves feedstock characterization, sorting, washing, drying, process control, guarding, maintenance, repeatability, regulatory obligations, product development, distribution, and customers. A workshop can learn a great deal by making a plastic sheet or molded object; that demonstration alone does not establish a safe or viable manufacturing business.

The following sections distinguish the **public claim**, the **concern raised**, and **what evidence would settle the question**. Related issues are addressed together rather than repeating the same criticism under every slogan.

## I. Openness, infrastructure, and control

### 1. “Free and open source”

Precious Plastic presents its designs and knowledge as freely available for people to use, modify, and share. Yet builders have reported incomplete fabrication packages, uncertain licensing, inconsistent versions, and some material or services subject to payment or restrictions. Public visibility is not the same as genuinely reusable open hardware.

A defensible open-source claim requires a clear license for each relevant asset, complete editable source files, CAD and drawings sufficient for reproduction, firmware and control logic where applicable, bills of materials, and traceable revisions. If essential files are unavailable or commercial use is restricted, those limitations should be stated prominently—not discovered after an investment has been made.

### 2. “It is a platform”

An effective platform enables independent contributors to publish, correct, extend, and preserve knowledge. In years of support and development work, the author encountered persistent difficulties communicating updates and correcting public information. Proposals to use established open-source community infrastructure, including Discourse, were reportedly rejected in favor of a custom platform incorporating proprietary dependencies such as Discord and Supabase.

Proprietary services do not inherently make a project illegitimate. The relevant questions are whether data and contributions are portable, moderation is accountable, material can be corrected, and contributors can participate without relying on the discretion of a small group. The author reports that technical objections and requests for improvements were sometimes met with dismissive responses, removal of content, or exclusion. Moderation records, platform policies, and version history would provide a basis for assessing those accounts.

### 3. “Earnings go to the community”

The project has promoted a narrative in which revenue and support sustain collective work. Its [community-platform repository](https://github.com/ONEARMY/community-platform/) describes years of collaboration with thousands of people on open-hardware projects. The author challenges both the scale implied by that description and the visibility of money returned to the people producing technical work.

In particular, the author disputes references to annual project or platform costs around **€300,000**, alleges that direct operating costs for the platform were far lower, and reports that a principal contributor to the original community platform received **less than €200**. These are substantial financial assertions that should not be treated as established without budgets, contracts, payment records, and a definition of what the claimed annual expenditure covers. Hosting costs alone, for example, cannot be equated with an organization's total cost of operations.

A credible answer would include itemized annual accounts, compensation and contractor payments, infrastructure expenses, grants, marketplace revenue, and a transparent account of how the work of external contributors is supported and credited. The author also recounts paying **€50** for a direct call with Dave Hakkens, an experience that heightened concerns about the relationship between public community messaging and access to decision-makers. That experience is evidence of the author's encounter, not proof of a broader financial conclusion.

### 4. “Transparent”

Transparency is more than publishing promotional updates. The concerns described here include unclear financial allocation, undocumented design changes, opaque decisions about listings and moderation, and little visible response to substantive technical feedback.

The relevant standard is straightforward: public budgets and decision records; dated roadmaps; identifiable maintainers; revision histories; published rules for moderation, financial interests, and vendor selection; and meaningful opportunities to appeal decisions. Without those records, outsiders cannot reliably distinguish a community initiative from a centrally managed brand.

### 5. “Community-driven governance”

Participants can contribute time, expertise, and content without possessing any meaningful influence over the rules under which those contributions are used. The author alleges that branding, marketplace visibility, and publication decisions remain concentrated among a small group, with favored commercial sellers enjoying disproportionate influence.

The question is not whether a project has administrators—every functioning platform needs governance—but whether the exercise of that authority is consistent, reviewable, and subject to checks. Published criteria for listing, delisting, moderation, conflicts of interest, and appeals would make the distinction measurable.

### 6. “Decentralized, and in your hands”

The rhetoric of decentralization sits uneasily beside a platform in which central administrators can control discoverability, remove contributions, and determine access to markets. The author reports substantial rejected or removed material, changes to marketplace conditions, and sharp declines in outside participation following later platform changes. A figure of **approximately 1,200** excluded or censored contributions appears in the underlying accounts; it requires an identifiable counting method and records before it can be cited as a verified total.

Decentralization should be evaluated by actual rights: whether contributors retain their files and customer relationships, can host independent mirrors, can challenge decisions, and can compete with recommended sellers on equal terms. A machine design may be in a user's possession while the surrounding distribution infrastructure remains centrally controlled.

## II. Engineering, safety, and documentation

### 7. “Easy to build”

The advertised simplicity of the machinery can obscure the skills and resources necessary to produce a reliable unit. Accurate machining, welding, fit and alignment, thermal design, wiring, tolerances, and the selection of suitable components are not minor finishing details. Outdated bills of materials or undocumented substitutions can turn a nominally accessible build into a lengthy engineering exercise.

Prospective builders should be given complete drawings, tolerances, current part numbers, tooling requirements, expected labor hours, and a realistic account of commissioning and troubleshooting. Some designs may be suitable learning projects, but they should not be represented as turnkey construction for inexperienced operators.

### 8. “Safe to use”

The safety concerns are among the most consequential in this record. Builders report insufficient machine guarding, accessible moving parts, inconsistent emergency-stop implementation, questionable heater arrangements, and the absence of documented risk assessments. The author further alleges that safety-related criticism and proposed revisions were suppressed in some official channels.

Shredders, heated barrels, presses, rotating shafts, electrical controls, and moving blades can cause severe injury or fire. A public design should clearly identify which protections are included, what remains the builder's responsibility, and whether any conformity claim has been independently established. Where applicable, evaluation should consider **ISO 12100**, **ISO 13857**, **IEC 60204-1**, and relevant jurisdiction-specific machinery requirements. Citing a standard does not establish compliance with it.

Until complete documentation and testing are available, these machines should be approached as prototypes requiring professional assessment—not presumed safe merely because they are widely shared or sold.

### 9. “HQ develops recycling solutions”

The project's public communications describe sustained development. The author, drawing on years of daily technical work, reports finding comparatively little official engineering output that could be independently reproduced and evaluated: limited test data, sparse change histories, incomplete safety records, and designs still requiring substantial correction by outside builders.

That judgment should be tested against dated repositories and releases, named engineering contributions, performance reports, field trials, and documented design revisions. Development is not measured by the number of videos or years associated with a brand. It is demonstrated by improved machines, reproducible performance, and resolved defects.

### 10. “Blueprints and business plans get you to market”

A drawing is not a validated production process. It does not supply tolerances for finished goods, repeatable quality control, machine conformity documentation, a supply chain, or buyers. The author reports encountering workshops that invested in equipment but failed to reach sustained production, including accounts of **€10,000–€30,000** purchases ultimately yielding little commercial value.

The original material also cites claimed failure or exit rates of **90–99%**. No complete cohort data is supplied to establish that percentage; it should be treated as an allegation requiring verification rather than a measured industry statistic. A meaningful test would track a defined set of buyers over time: who remains operational, what they manufacture, at what throughput, and with what net results.

### 11. “The library contains the knowledge”

A knowledge library is only as useful as its accuracy, completeness, and maintenance. The author describes a substantial gap between the accessible public materials and the engineering knowledge required in practice, alleging outdated files, missing revisions, removed contributions, and inadequate treatment of failures. A claim that the library represents roughly **5%** of the available practical knowledge is an impression in the source material, not a verified measurement.

Filament production is one example: an attractive sample spool is not proof of commercially reliable diameter control, moisture management, mechanical properties, or repeatable output. The same applies to sheets, molded parts, and shredded feedstock. Each process needs measurable acceptance criteria and a maintained record of experiments, failures, and corrections.

## III. Business claims, market realities, and industrial comparisons

### 12. “Make money with these machines”

Selling the possibility of an income imposes a higher evidentiary obligation than demonstrating a process. Throughput, contamination, scrap, labor, utilities, rent, maintenance, tooling, customer acquisition, certification, packaging, and rejected products determine whether a workshop survives—not the selling price of an isolated decorative object.

The author estimates that a realistic small commercial operation can require **€100,000–€200,000 in equipment** plus substantial further labor and operating expenditure, potentially **€100,000–€300,000** in certain scenarios. Those are contextual estimates, not universal minimums. They illustrate how misleading a machine-only startup price can be.

Anyone promoting profitability should publish operating cases with actual invoices, material yields, working hours, customer orders, and fully loaded costs, including the operator's wage.

### 13. “Make it your job”

A professional occupation must provide more than an identity. It requires a service or product with customers, skills that can be taught and assessed, safe operating procedures, and income that remains after expenses. The author reports numerous participants learning that a busy workshop or social-media following did not translate into sufficient sales or wages.

The important question is not whether some people work in recycling—they clearly do—but whether this specific machinery and business model support lasting employment. Case studies should distinguish subsidized education and demonstration projects from self-supporting manufacturing enterprises.

### 14. “Plastic is a precious resource”

Recovered plastic can have economic value, but value depends on polymer type, cleanliness, consistency, local demand, and the cost of preparation. Sorting, washing, drying, and reprocessing are labor- and energy-intensive. Contaminated, mixed, or unidentified feedstock may have little commercial value and can introduce process and exposure hazards.

The source material cites examples of low-value bricks, labor-intensive plant pots, and experimental beams that did not become repeatable commercial products. It mentions a press around **€3,000**, molds in the **€500–€1,000** range, and roughly **twenty minutes per small molded item** in one example. These figures need machine- and process-specific confirmation, but they identify the right economic test: total cost and saleable output per hour, not the rhetorical value assigned to waste.

### 15. “Better and cheaper than industrial equipment”

The author disputes the value of several officially recommended machines relative to used or purpose-built industrial alternatives. Examples in the underlying account include a shredder allegedly rising from approximately **€180** to **€4,900**, alternatives around **€4,000** with substantially higher throughput, and shredders later advertised in the **€8,000–€15,000** range. Extruders, injection units, and presses are likewise criticized for high prices relative to duty cycle and missing downstream equipment.

The sheet press is a particularly prominent case. The author alleges insufficient pressure and temperature uniformity, structural limitations, costly operation, and difficulty producing market-grade sheets. A cited operating scenario includes a press around **€15,000**, an additional **€20,000–€50,000** in equipment, significant floor space, and **five to eight hours per sheet**. The source also describes a design with approximately **16 kW** of installed heating capacity. These numbers are illustrative claims from the author; they should be reconciled with exact model specifications and dated supplier quotations before being used as direct comparisons.

A fair comparison requires **matched inputs and outputs**: price, included equipment, throughput (kg/h), energy per kilogram, labor, defect rate, maintenance, guarding, available spare parts, and service life. A lower purchase price is not a saving if the equipment cannot perform the intended work; conversely, not every educational machine needs to match industrial throughput. The intended use must be stated honestly.

### 16. “Used by Siemens, Coca-Cola, and other major brands”

References to prominent industrial companies can create an impression of technical endorsement. Yet a logo, mention, workshop interaction, or promotional photograph is not evidence that a corporation operates, purchases, or recommends a particular machine. The author reports unsuccessful attempts by some workshops to turn supposed brand interest into actual collaborations.

Claims of industrial adoption should identify the nature of the relationship: sponsorship, workshop, prototype trial, material donation, formal purchase, or sustained production deployment. Strong evidence would include a named application, the machine specification, purchase or trial documentation, operating results, and authorization to cite the company. No conclusion about any named company's involvement should be drawn from the present account alone.

## IV. Impact, participation, and the ownership of work

### 17. “Sustainable impact at scale”

An environmental claim requires an environmental measurement. Processing a small quantity of recovered plastic can provide educational value; it does not automatically demonstrate net climate benefit, material displacement, or significant waste diversion. Small batch sizes, contamination, electricity consumption, transport, additives, and low-value end products can materially change the result.

The author criticizes impact statements that lack transparent throughput, reject rates, and life-cycle assumptions. Verifiable reporting should distinguish waste collected from waste actually converted into durable, saleable material; disclose energy use and processing losses; and compare the outcome with realistic industrial and local alternatives.

### 18. “We will fix the plastic problem”

Plastic pollution cannot be resolved through machine ownership alone. Collection systems, product design, chemical composition, market prices, regulatory requirements, and consumption patterns operate on scales far beyond an individual workshop. Presenting a set of small machines as a comprehensive answer risks encouraging investment without acknowledging those conditions.

The author's objection is to the distance between moral urgency in promotional language and the measurable limits of the proposed intervention. The project may encourage learning and local experimentation. Its environmental contribution, however, should be expressed in evidence—not promises of world-changing impact unsupported by mass balances and operating data.

### 19. “Thirteen years of work; 80,000 people; 1,000 workspaces”

Years associated with a brand are not automatically years worked by its present leadership. Similarly, follower counts, registrations, Discord membership, and pins on a map do not establish current, productive workshops. The original record questions figures of roughly **80,000 members**, **1,000 workspaces**, and **10,000 Discord users**, alleging that many listed spaces are inactive or unrelated, and that actual sustained participation is much smaller. It cites estimates of around **1,300 map pins**, roughly **1,500 rejected pins**, and only tens of viable operations; none is an audited count in the material provided.

The appropriate response is not another headline number but an auditable census: clearly defined membership categories, deduplicated accounts, current workspace status, dates of activity, verified production, and consent from organizations appearing on the map.

The same principle applies to attribution. The author alleges that historical work by volunteers has sometimes been presented as the continuing achievement of the current organization or of individuals who did not originate the underlying designs. In particular, the source disputes the attribution of the sheet-press development in an October 2026 post, [“10 years into one post”](https://www.reddit.com/r/PreciousPlastic/comments/1x1lk49/10_years_into_one_post/), linked to the video [“We finally found a way to turn plastic waste into sheets”](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KlWkAaP_sR8). It identifies **Jason** as the person who should receive principal credit and refers to other contributors, including **Michael**, as no longer involved. This is an attribution dispute best resolved with original drawings, commit histories, dated prototypes, correspondence, and acknowledgment from the individuals concerned.

Some allegations additionally concern marketplace vendors or moderators, including **MadPlastic**, **Sustainable Design Studio**, and the marketplace administration. The author asserts that people who joined later acquired influence over rules and exposure while the original contributors lost recognition. Claims that specific vendors copied designs, manipulated rankings, misused client messages, or acted improperly must be assessed individually against original documents and records; they should not be treated as proved by association with the broader critique.

### 20. “A community that empowers its contributors”

This is the issue that connects the technical and institutional criticisms. The author describes years of answering questions, working with builders, proposing corrections, and attempting to improve public resources—then encountering exclusion, hostile moderation, or the disappearance of contributions. Other accounts collected by the author describe similar frustrations.

Such experiences cannot, on their own, establish the conduct of every moderator or volunteer. They do, however, raise a serious governance question: **what happens when a contributor presents evidence that a machine is unsafe, economically misrepresented, or technically defective?** A healthy engineering community should retain the report, investigate it, publish a decision, credit useful corrections, and permit independent disagreement. If instead feedback is removed or critics lose access, the project deprives future builders of information they need to make safe and informed choices.

## Patterns that require answers

Across these twenty claims, several recurring problems deserve focused examination:

- **Design completeness:** missing drawings, unclear tolerances, outdated components, and incomplete fabrication or control files.
- **Safety evidence:** absence of accessible risk assessments, test records, revisions, and clearly assigned conformity responsibilities.
- **Measured performance:** limited independent data for throughput, energy use, reliability, product quality, and waste rejection.
- **Business viability:** promotional cost figures that exclude substantial equipment, labor, overhead, and customer-development expenses.
- **Contributor treatment:** contested attribution, limited appeal mechanisms, and allegations of removed technical criticism.
- **Financial and editorial accountability:** unclear allocation of funds, conflicts of interest, marketplace selection criteria, and ownership of platform decisions.

These are not peripheral complaints. They bear directly on physical safety, financial exposure, and whether users can trust the knowledge presented to them.

## What readers should verify before investing

Before buying or constructing a machine, request the complete design package and identify its actual license. Examine manufacturing tolerances, wiring, component specifications, and documented revisions. Obtain a machine-specific risk assessment and confirm which guards, interlocks, temperature protections, and emergency controls are present. Determine the legal responsibilities of the machine builder, seller, installer, and operator in the intended jurisdiction.

Ask for repeatable performance tests, not carefully selected demonstrations. A production claim should include material type and condition, output per hour, electrical consumption, rejection rate, maintenance, labor, and the quality standards applied to the finished article. For a business, obtain real purchase orders or sales data and calculate results after all costs, including wages and taxes.

Where impact or community scale is invoked, request the underlying methodology and records. When prominent businesses or historical contributors are named, verify the precise relationship and attribution. Keep copies of quotations, specifications, listings, correspondence, and revisions: claims change over time, and dates matter.

## What meaningful reform would look like

Precious Plastic could address many of these concerns with concrete steps: publish complete and consistently licensed designs; maintain versioned, testable engineering releases; commission independent safety reviews; publish performance and economic case studies; disclose accounts and vendor relationships; and institute documented moderation and appeal procedures. Historical contributors should receive traceable credit, and critical corrections should remain available unless there is a documented reason for removal.

These measures would not guarantee that small-scale recycling becomes profitable or environmentally superior. They would, however, allow builders, educators, donors, and buyers to make decisions based on evidence rather than promotional trust.

Additional reference points include the [ONEARMY community-platform repository](https://github.com/ONEARMY/community-platform/), the [October 2026 Reddit discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/PreciousPlastic/comments/1x1lk49/10_years_into_one_post/), and the associated [sheet-press video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KlWkAaP_sR8). These sources have different evidentiary weight; commentary, screenshots, public statements, technical files, and financial records should not be conflated.

This article is a critical record, not a court finding or a completed independent audit. Some statements derive from firsthand experience; others concern accounts supplied by third parties or numerical estimates yet to be substantiated. Anyone named or implicated is invited to provide documents, corrections, technical reports, financial statements, or a specific response. Supported corrections should be incorporated with a visible revision history, not silently erased.

## Conclusion

Precious Plastic has asked people to trust a vision of open engineering, local manufacturing, community participation, and environmental benefit. Trust is consequential when it leads people to build potentially hazardous machinery, spend savings, start businesses, or donate labor.

The measure of the project is therefore not the force of its slogans, the age of its brand, or the reach of its videos. It is the quality of the machines, the completeness of the documentation, the honesty of its commercial and environmental claims, and the fairness with which it treats the people whose work made the project possible.

**If those claims are sound, the records should demonstrate it. If they are not, prospective builders and supporters deserve to know before they bear the cost.**
